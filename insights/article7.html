Consolidating Banking and Payment Reporting: The Family Office's Other Data Problem

Portfolio reporting gets the attention and the budget. But the money leaves through bank accounts — dozens of them, at banks that never agreed on a format — and the payment report is where a family office's control actually lives or dies.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In March 2021, Gabriele Gallotti, founder of the Geneva family office Novum Capital, told Citywire something that should have stopped every family office COO in their tracks. His firm, managing CHF 1.6 billion for 14 families, was dealing with about 220 banking relationships — individual accounts, company accounts, children's accounts, spread across more than 30 jurisdictions. Asset consolidation, he said, was the service his clients needed most, and the only way to do it well was in-house. Five years later, the number is the story: 220 relationships is not an outlier. It is what a multi-generational family with operating businesses, trusts, and accounts in several countries accumulates when nobody is ever told to close an account.

That accumulation is the family office's other data problem. Consolidated portfolio reporting — the subject of this site's second article — is the hard problem everyone talks about: six to ten custodians and private banks, alternatives arriving quarterly on PDFs already 45 to 90 days stale, entities stacked inside entities. Banking and payment reporting is the problem underneath it, and it is harder in one specific way. A portfolio report describes what the family owns. A payment report describes what the family did — every wire, every ACH, every check, every fee — and it has to be right before the money moves, not after. Get the portfolio report wrong and the committee sees a stale number. Get the payment report wrong and the money is gone.

The sprawl is the story

The counts pile up fast. A substantial family office holds assets at six to ten custodians and private banks, each reporting in its own format on its own schedule. The bank-account count runs higher than the custodian count: practitioner guides describe family offices reconciling 50, 100 or more bank accounts every month, each one a separate statement, a separate login, a separate fee schedule. Novum's 220 relationships across 14 families works out to roughly 16 per family — and that is a firm whose entire pitch is consolidation. Deloitte counted 8,030 single family offices worldwide in 2024 and projects 10,720 by 2030, with assets under management rising from $3.1 trillion to $5.4 trillion over the same period. Every one of those offices adds accounts faster than it closes them, because opening an account takes a signature and closing one takes a committee decision, a tax review, and a conversation with a banker who would rather keep the relationship.

The cost of the sprawl is not the account fees, though those add up. It is the reconciliation labor and the control gaps. The offices that produce a trustworthy consolidated portfolio report typically dedicate one to three full-time professionals to data integrity — and that staffing covers the investment side. The banking side needs the same discipline: matching every bank feed against the general ledger, chasing the fee that does not tie to the statement, catching the duplicate payment before it clears twice. UBS and Campden's older cost tables remain the only public basis-point breakdown of what offices spend: 86 basis points of assets on average, nearly half of it on investment activities, with a 2017 total of 118.3 basis points. The software license is perhaps 30 percent of the total cost of consolidated reporting. The rest is people doing the unglamorous work of making the data right — and on the banking side, most offices have not hired those people yet.

Payments are not positions

The reason banking and payment reporting resists the portfolio-reporting toolkit is that a payment is an instruction, not a position. A holding sits still and gets repriced. A payment moves, and once it moves it cannot be un-moved. A consolidated payment report has to answer four questions for every item: who authorized it, who received it, which entity it came from, and what it cost. None of those questions is answered by a portfolio system. The rails underneath make the scale plain. In 2024 the ACH Network processed 33.6 billion payments valued at $86.2 trillion, up 6.7 percent in volume and 7.6 percent in value on 2023, according to Nacha. Same Day ACH alone topped 1.2 billion payments worth $3.2 trillion, and business-to-business ACH volume grew 11.6 percent. A family office does not touch a meaningful fraction of that flow, but it touches every rail it uses — wire for the large and the urgent, ACH for the recurring, check for the vendor who still insists on paper — and each rail reports differently, settles differently, and fails differently.

A portfolio report is a valuation. A payment report is a control. The first can be a week late. The second cannot.

The fraud arithmetic

The case for consolidating payment reporting is not efficiency. It is fraud, and the numbers are blunt. The Association for Financial Professionals' 2025 Payments Fraud and Control Survey, underwritten by Truist and based on 521 corporate treasury practitioners reporting on 2024, found that 79 percent of organizations were victims of attempted or actual payments fraud, barely down from 80 percent in 2023. Business email compromise was the number one avenue, cited by 63 percent of respondents. Checks were the payment method most often subjected to fraud, at 63 percent — and 75 percent of respondents have no plans to eliminate check use in the next two years. Wire transfers were the payment method most frequently targeted by BEC scammers, reported by 63 percent of respondents, up from 39 percent the previous year. Only 22 percent of organizations were able to recover 75 percent or more of the funds lost. Read those numbers through a family office's door and the exposure sharpens: a corporation loses a five-figure invoice payment; a family office loses a seven-figure wire to a contractor, a tax authority, or a capital call — and the person who approved it may be the same person who entered it.

Vendor discipline is the quiet half of the fraud problem. Vendor imposter fraud — a fraudster posing as a known supplier to redirect a payment — was reported by 45 percent of respondents in the 2025 AFP survey, up from 34 percent the year before. A family office pays an unusually wide vendor base: property managers, attorneys, art shippers, aircraft operators, school bursars, household staff agencies. Each one is a payee record, a bank detail, and an attack surface. A consolidated payment report that does not include a controlled vendor master — one approved payee list, with bank details verified by a second channel before the first payment — is a report of what happened, not a control over what happens next.

What consolidation actually means

Consolidating banking vendor reporting comes down to three moves, and only the third is a software purchase. First, rationalize the vendor count. Every banking relationship needs a stated reason — a jurisdiction, a lending line, a custody arrangement, a trust situs — or it goes on a closure list with a date. The test is not whether the bank is good; most of them are. The test is whether the family can say, for each one, what it does that no other relationship does. Second, draw one rail map. Which payments go by wire, which by ACH, which by check, and why. The check population deserves particular attention: checks are the most fraud-targeted payment method in the AFP data, yet families keep writing them for the vendors who prefer paper — and every check is a routing number and account number handed to a stranger. Third, produce one consolidated payment report: every account, every payment, every fee, reconciled daily against the ledger, with dual approval on wires above a threshold and positive pay on every check account. The platforms in this site's other articles — Addepar, Masttro, Private Wealth Systems — handle the portfolio side of the stack. The payment side is usually a treasury workstation, a bank portal, or a spreadsheet, and the offices that get it right staff it the way they staff portfolio reconciliation: as a permanent function, not a project.

The counterargument: some sprawl is deliberate

There is a real case against consolidation, and it starts with concentration. A family that banks with one institution has one point of failure, and deposit insurance covers $250,000 per depositor per bank — a rounding error against a family office's operating cash. Multiple banks also mean multiple credit lines, multiple private bankers, and negotiating leverage that disappears the moment the family becomes a single-bank client. Jurisdiction is the third reason: a family with assets, beneficiaries, and tax exposure in several countries may need local banking in each one, and no single institution is best everywhere. The honest version of consolidation is not one bank. It is a deliberate number of banks, each with a written reason, each feeding one consolidated report, and each reviewed annually against the question that opened this article: does anyone actually know, in one place, what went out?

The verdict

The test for a family office's banking and payment reporting is simple enough to run by noon tomorrow. Produce one report showing every payment out of every account in the last 30 days — who authorized each one, who received it, which entity it came from, and what it cost in fees. If the office can produce it, the banking vendors are consolidated, whatever the count. If it takes a week, three spreadsheets, and a phone call to a relationship manager, they are not consolidated; they are merely numerous. Portfolio reporting tells the family what it owns. Banking and payment reporting tells the family what it did — and whether anyone could have stopped it. The first is the report the committee reads. The second is the report that keeps the money.
