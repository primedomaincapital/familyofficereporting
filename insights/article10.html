Quarterly Reporting vs. Real-Time Dashboards: What Families Actually Read

The quarterly report arrives on the fifteenth of the month, forty pages thick, and the family reads it the way families always have: the principal scans the first page, the next generation skips to the performance table, and nobody reads the appendix. Meanwhile, on a phone in the same room, the real-time dashboard shows the portfolio's value as of this morning — and it is already out of date, because the private equity funds that make up 44% of the portfolio report quarterly, with a lag of one to two quarters. The dashboard is real-time. The portfolio is not. That gap is the whole argument about family office reporting, and it is larger than the software vendors admit.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

UBS's Global Family Office Report 2025, based on a survey of 317 family offices conducted from January 22 to April 4, 2025 — families with an average net worth of $2.7 billion, whose offices manage $1.1 billion each — puts the numbers on the table. The average family office portfolio held 44% in alternatives in 2024, up from 42% in 2023, with private equity at 21% of the total — 11% in direct investments and 10% in funds. Direct investments now account for more than 40% of the typical family office private equity allocation. Strategic asset allocation is performed in-house by 86% of offices, financial reporting by 75%, and portfolio risk management by 73%. Most investment committees meet monthly or quarterly, and 61% of offices have one, with an average of four family members sitting on it. The family office is, in other words, an institution that reports like an institution — and holds a portfolio that cannot be reported like one.

What the dashboard actually shows

The real-time dashboard is the product the vendors sell hardest, and it is genuinely useful for the part of the portfolio it can see. Addepar, the largest platform in the space, reports about $10 trillion across more than 1,500 firms as of October 2026. SS&C Black Diamond reports 3,300-plus firms and $4.6 trillion-plus in assets. Both sync with Schwab, Fidelity, and Pershing, reconcile positions to custodian statements, and present a consolidated balance sheet across every account, entity, and currency. For the liquid half of the portfolio — the public equities, the bonds, the cash — the dashboard is accurate to the day, and sometimes to the hour. A family that wants to know what it owns right now can know.

But the liquid half is the smaller half. With 44% of the average portfolio in alternatives, and private equity alone at 21%, the dashboard's real-time number is a composite of today's market prices and last quarter's fund valuations — and the fund valuations are themselves estimates, reported with a lag and revised later. The dashboard does not hide this. It marks the private holdings as stale, or carries them at cost, or shows the last reported value with a date stamp. The family reads the number anyway. The dashboard's precision is the problem: a number shown to the dollar invites a confidence the underlying data does not support.

The cost of the dashboard is the second surprise. Addepar is priced at 0.8 to 3 basis points on assets, with an average contract of $229,000 according to Sacra, and reported pricing of $50,000 to $300,000-plus a year for large books. Masttro runs $50,000 to $150,000-plus. Eton Solutions starts at $150,000-plus. At the other end, Asora charges about $900 a month and Aleta about $1,000 a month for reporting. Implementation runs 30 to 90 days at the light end and three to nine months at the heavy end — Addepar's own implementations stretch to six to twelve months for complex, multi-custodian, alternatives-heavy books. The dashboard is not a subscription. It is a project.

There is a third problem, and it is the one the vendors least like to discuss: the dashboard is only as honest as the data feeding it, and the data feeding it is only as honest as the slowest source. The liquid positions reconcile daily against custodian statements — Schwab, Fidelity, Pershing — and the dashboard shows them accurately. The alternatives reconcile quarterly, when the fund administrators send their statements, and the dashboard carries the last reported value in the meantime. The family office's reporting analyst does not spend the day reading the dashboard. The analyst spends the day reconciling it — chasing the fund statement that arrived late, the capital call that was not yet recorded, the distribution that was booked to the wrong entity. Addepar's Financial Graph, which models fund hierarchies and waterfalls, exists precisely because that reconciliation is hard, and it is part of why the average contract costs $229,000. The dashboard does not eliminate the reconciliation work. It concentrates it, and it charges for the privilege.

What the quarterly report does that the dashboard cannot

The quarterly report is slower, thicker, and — for the decisions a family actually makes — more useful. It is the document the investment committee reads before it votes, and it is the document the next generation reads when it inherits. Its virtue is not speed. It is judgment. The quarterly report carries the narrative the dashboard cannot: why the allocation changed, what the manager said on the call, whether the direct deal's thesis still holds, and what the family intends to do about the position that has not moved in eighteen months. The dashboard shows the value. The report shows the reasoning.

That distinction is why the UBS numbers matter. When 75% of family offices perform financial reporting in-house, they are not outsourcing the narrative — they are keeping it. The report is the family's own account of its decisions, written by people who were in the room. A vendor's dashboard can aggregate the positions and compute the returns. It cannot write the paragraph that explains why the family sold the position at a loss, or why it is holding the one that has not performed. The quarterly report is where the family's judgment lives, and it is the document that survives the people who wrote it.

The counterargument deserves its weight, because the dashboard's defenders have a real point. A family that waits for the quarterly report to discover that a manager has drifted from its mandate, or that a position has breached its risk limit, has waited too long. The dashboard's value is not the number on the screen. It is the alert — the breach, the drift, the concentration — that fires the day it happens rather than the quarter it is reported. For a family with 44% in alternatives and direct deals that move on their own schedule, the dashboard is the early-warning system the quarterly report cannot be. A family that reads only the quarterly report is flying with a three-month lag, and the lag is the risk.

But the early-warning argument has a limit, and the limit is the alternatives themselves. A dashboard can alert on a public equity position that has moved 10% in a week. It cannot alert on a private equity fund whose valuation has not changed because it has not been reported. The 44% of the portfolio that is alternatives does not move on the dashboard's clock, and no amount of real-time technology changes that. The dashboard is an early-warning system for the half of the portfolio that least needs one, and a rear-view mirror for the half that does.

For a family office, the practical conclusion is to run both, and to keep their roles separate. Use the dashboard for what it is good at: the consolidated balance sheet, the liquid positions, the alerts on public-market drift. Use the quarterly report for what it is good at: the narrative, the judgment, the decisions, and the record. Do not let the dashboard's precision colonize the report's judgment, and do not let the report's slowness excuse the absence of a dashboard. And for anyone building reporting software, the missing product is now obvious. The dashboard that marks stale valuations honestly, shows the confidence interval on every number rather than a single figure, and writes the first draft of the quarterly narrative from the data it already holds will own the next decade of family office reporting. The family does not need a faster number. It needs a truer one.
