What Does Family Office Reporting Software Cost?

Nobody publishes a price list for family office reporting software. The reported ranges run from about $12,000 a year for a flat-fee platform to $300,000 for an Addepar-scale deployment — and the license is the smallest line on the invoice.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In February 2026, the chief operating officer of a $900 million single-family office walked into a budget meeting carrying three numbers for the same purchase. One vendor had quoted $48,000 a year. The second had quoted $210,000. The third had refused to quote at all until the office handed over its entity chart, its custodian list, and two years of statements. All three were selling the same thing — a consolidated view of what the family owns — and none of the three numbers meant the same thing.

That meeting is the normal experience of buying family office reporting software in 2026, and the confusion is structural. Nobody in this market publishes a price list. Addepar, Masttro, Private Wealth Systems, and SEI Archway — the platforms that run the largest family offices — all quote privately, priced on assets, entities, feeds, and modules. The challengers that do publish prices — Aleta from $1,000 a month, Asora from about $900 a month, MYFO from about $850 a month — sell a narrower scope to a smaller office, so the numbers do not compare directly. Even the size of the market is disputed: one research firm puts family office software at $1.16 billion in 2026, another at $5.3 billion in 2025. The gap is definitional — what counts as family office software — and it is a warning about every number that follows.

Four lines on the invoice

Every purchase is four bills, and the quote usually shows one. First, the license: the platform subscription. Second, implementation: migration, feed setup, report templates, training. Third, the data feeds and add-ons: the per-account and per-fund charges that scale with the family's sprawl. Fourth, the people: the staff who run the system and reconcile what it produces. This site’s earlier reporting put the shape of the bill plainly: the software license is perhaps 30 percent of the total cost of consolidated reporting, with the rest going to the people doing the work of making the data right. UBS and Campden’s cost tables, the only public basis-point breakdown of what offices spend, put the average office’s total operating cost at 86 basis points of assets, nearly half of it on investment activities, with a 2017 total of 118.3 basis points. The license is the smallest of the four.

Line one: the license

Addepar sets the top of the market, and its pricing is the most studied precisely because the company never publishes it. Founded in 2009 by Joe Lonsdale and now valued at over $2 billion after its 2024 funding rounds, Addepar serves more than 1,400 firms in about 60 countries with over $9 trillion of assets on the platform as of early 2026. Third-party guides put the entry point for a single family office at roughly $50,000 to $70,000 a year before implementation, with contracts scaling on assets at roughly 0.8 to 3 basis points. Sacra, the private-markets research firm, reported an average contract value of $229,000 per customer in 2024. The arithmetic is what shocks first-time buyers: at 1.5 basis points, a $2 billion book implies a $300,000 annual fee — and the fee rises every year the markets do, whether the office uses more software or not.

Masttro prices the other way: a fixed annual subscription, not based on assets under management, quoted privately with no published figures. An office whose assets double pays Masttro the same license and Addepar roughly twice as much. Private Wealth Systems and SEI Archway quote privately as well, and their buyers report the same experience: the real number arrives after a sales process, not before it.

The published-price challengers are explicit about the trade. Aleta prices from $1,000 a month, based on platform scope rather than assets, with more than 100 custodian and bank integrations maintained by its own data team. Asora prices in tiers by family net worth from about $900 a month, with its published tiers cited at $15,600 a year for a family in the $50 million to $100 million bracket. MYFO starts at about $850 a month with no implementation fees. These are real prices for real products — and they buy a narrower scope: fewer entities, fewer feeds, lighter alternatives modeling. The comparison that matters is license against the office’s entity count.

Line two: implementation

Implementation is priced as a project, and its size depends on a fact nobody knows before the project starts: how dirty the data is. Asora is the rare vendor that publishes the formula — a one-time implementation fee of 25 percent of the annual subscription, paid at the start of onboarding. For Addepar, the independent guides put standard onboarding at roughly 60 to 90 days, with a streamlined AddeparGO route near 30 days, and six to twelve months for a complex book. This site’s own evaluation of Addepar put the honest figure higher still: implementations routinely take 12 to 18 months for a complex family structure, not the three to six months vendors project. The spread between 30 days and 18 months is not a disagreement about software. It is a disagreement about the family.

Line three: the data feeds

The feeds are where the per-unit pricing hides, and they are the line most budgets miss entirely. Asora’s published schedule makes the shape visible: the first 20 investment accounts are included, then $3,600 a year per extra pack of 20; alternative fund holdings cost another $3,600 a year per pack of 20 through its Canoe Intelligence add-on; a private cloud adds $4,000 a year. Canoe itself retrieves documents from more than 3,000 general-partner and administrator portals, which is the scale of the alternatives problem in one number. Masttro advertises more than 750 direct data-feed connections. A family with 60 accounts and 40 funds pays for the packs; a family with 12 accounts does not. The custodian feeds themselves are usually free to receive — the cost is the vendor’s work normalizing them.

Line four: the people

The people line is the budget, and it is the line most often left out of the vendor-led total entirely. The offices that produce a trustworthy consolidated report typically dedicate one to three full-time professionals to data integrity, and that staffing covers the investment side alone. The salary band is not mysterious: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics put the median annual wage for information security analysts — a reasonable proxy for the technical staff these roles require — at $129,180 in May 2025, before benefits, tools, or management overhead. Addepar’s edge, as this site’s evaluation argued, is partly its data operation — the teams that chase administrators, reconcile breaks, and model the uncertainty. Buy the cheaper platform without that operation and the office hires the operation itself, at salary rather than subscription.

What the bands look like

Add the four lines together and the totals fall into bands. A small office — $50 million to $250 million, one or two entities, mostly liquid holdings, under 20 accounts — should plan on $12,000 to $36,000 a year for the license, an implementation fee near a quarter of that, and no extra feed packs: $15,000 to $50,000 in the first year and $12,000 to $40,000 a year to run. A mid-size office — $250 million to $1 billion, several entities, 20 to 60 accounts, a dozen funds — lands at $50,000 to $150,000 a year for the license, with feed packs and an alternatives add-on on top: $75,000 to $200,000 in year one and $60,000 to $175,000 a year after. A large office — over $1 billion, heavy alternatives, many entities — should expect $150,000 to $300,000 or more a year for the license alone, with a first-year total of $250,000 to $500,000 once implementation and staffing are counted, and a run-rate of $200,000 to $400,000 before internal staff. Those bands are built from the component prices above, not taken from a survey: no vendor-neutral survey of total reporting-software cost exists.

The license is the quote. The people are the budget. The feeds are the surprise.

The honest counterargument

There is a serious case for paying the top of the range, and it starts with what the basis-point model buys. Addepar’s data operation — the normalization, the reconciliation, the chasing of administrators — is the product as much as the software is, and an office that buys the cheapest license and staffs nothing gets expensive dashboards over the same broken feeds. The AUM-based fee, for all its faults, aligns the vendor’s revenue with the family’s wealth. And price opacity has a defense: bespoke pricing lets a vendor discount a simple book instead of charging it the enterprise rate.

The case against spending at all is equally serious. Family offices control trillions — Deloitte counted 8,030 single family offices worldwide in 2024, managing an estimated $3.1 trillion, projected to reach 10,720 offices and $5.4 trillion by 2030 — and most of them still run on spreadsheets, as this site’s fourth article documented. A spreadsheet costs nothing, it never charges per feed, and the family that knows its own holdings can produce a quarterly report in three weeks of staff time. The honest answer to that case is the one the data gives: the spreadsheet works until the alternatives book, the entity count, or the succession timeline outgrows it. The price of the software is the price of outgrowing the spreadsheet — and the families that pay it are the ones whose portfolios already have.

What to do with the number

Ask every vendor for the four-line quote in writing: license, implementation, the feed and add-on schedule by the family’s actual account and fund counts, and the staffing the vendor assumes the office provides. Price the second year, not the first — implementation discounts vanish, AUM-based fees rise with the markets, and the feed packs arrive once the real account count is known. Run the test this site’s Addepar evaluation proposed: hand the vendor three capital account statements, a K-1, and the family’s entity chart, and ask for a consolidated view with look-through and a defensible IRR within a week. A $200,000 platform that retires three weeks of quarterly spreadsheet assembly and one full-time reconciliation hire is cheap. A $12,000 platform that nobody trusts enough to read is the most expensive software the family will ever buy.
