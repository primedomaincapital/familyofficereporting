Single vs. Multi-Family Office Technology Stacks Compared

Single-family offices build custom stacks. Multi-family offices need scale. How technology requirements diverge — and what each model should buy.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

A single-family office managing $2 billion and a multi-family office managing $2 billion across 40 families have almost nothing in common technologically — despite what software vendors, who sell to both with the same pitch deck, would have you believe. The requirements diverge on nearly every axis: data architecture, reporting, compliance, billing, and the fundamental question of whose interests the technology serves.

Understanding the divergence is essential whether you are building an SFO tech stack, evaluating an MFO, or selling software to either.

The Single-Family Office Stack: Depth Over Breadth

The SFO's technology problem is depth. One family, extraordinary complexity, no tolerance for approximation. The stack reflects this.

Core: the golden record. Addepar dominates this tier, with Masttro and a few others competing. The selection criterion is not features — it is whether the platform can model the family's actual structure: the dynasty trusts, the private foundation, the operating company, the offshore entities, the art. Implementations take six to eighteen months and cost $150,000 to $500,000 annually all-in (license plus data operations). Families that try to economize here invariably spend more fixing the consequences.

Satellites: best-of-breed point solutions. Around the core, SFOs assemble specialized tools: Canoe Intelligence for alternatives document processing, Archway or Private Wealth Systems for partnership accounting, a dedicated bill-pay system, a CRM (often Salesforce, customized heavily, or a purpose-built tool like Altvia), and increasingly a data warehouse (Snowflake is common) for custom analytics. Cybersecurity is a standalone line item — family offices are prime targets, and the good ones spend accordingly.

The defining characteristic of the SFO stack is customization. The family office exists to serve one family's idiosyncratic needs, and the technology follows. If the patriarch wants a dashboard showing the carbon intensity of the portfolio alongside performance, someone builds it. If the next generation wants an app for reviewing trust distributions, it gets built. The stack is a reflection of the family — which is both its strength and its fragility. When the long-tenured CTO retires, the bespoke integrations retire with them unless they were documented, which they weren't.

The Multi-Family Office Stack: Breadth Over Depth

The MFO's problem is the inverse: breadth. Forty families, each less complex than the SFO's one, but the technology must serve all of them from a common platform — profitably.

Core: the scalable platform. MFOs gravitate toward platforms that support multi-tenancy natively: separate data, separate reporting, shared infrastructure. Addepar serves this market too, but so do Black Diamond (SS&C), Orion, and Tamarac (Envestnet) — platforms built for the RIA market that MFOs adapt. The economics demand it: an MFO cannot spend $300,000 per family on technology implementation. The platform must onboard a new family in weeks, not quarters.

The billing imperative. This is the capability SFOs never think about and MFOs cannot live without. The MFO must calculate fees across families — typically basis points on AUM, often with tiered schedules, sometimes with performance components — generate invoices, track receivables, and report revenue by family, by advisor, by service line. The billing engine is as central to the M *FO stack as the reporting engine. Platforms without robust billing (which is most SFO-oriented tools) force MFOs into spreadsheet workarounds that auditors hate.

Compliance as infrastructure. An SFO operating under the family office exemption has minimal regulatory reporting. An MFO — typically a registered investment adviser — lives under SEC examination. The technology stack must support compliance natively: trade surveillance, personal trading monitoring, marketing rule compliance, Form ADV data, examination-ready audit trails. This isn't a feature; it's the price of admission, and it shapes every other technology decision.

Where the Models Are Converging

"The best SFOs are adopting MFO discipline. The best MFOs are adopting SFO depth. The technology is meeting in the middle."

Two trends are blurring the historical divide. First, SFOs are professionalizing. The generation that built wealth in operating businesses is ceding control to next-gen members and professional CIOs who demand institutional-grade operations — including the compliance infrastructure and standardized reporting that were once MFO-only concerns. The SFO tech stack is getting more disciplined.

Second, MFOs are segmenting. The leading multi-family offices now tier their technology: a standardized platform for the $10 million families, a deeper SFO-like stack for the $100 million families who demand it. This tiering is operationally complex but commercially necessary — the $100 million family will leave for an SFO (or start one) if the technology feels mass-market.

The technology vendors are responding. Addepar's product now spans both models. New entrants like Masttro were designed from inception for the complexity range between mass-affluent RIA tools and bespoke SFO builds. The API-first architecture — golden record plus custom presentation layer — serves both: the SFO builds its idiosyncratic dashboards on the API; the MFO builds standardized client portals on the same API.

The Buyer's Decision

For families choosing between establishing an SFO and joining an MFO, technology is a legitimate decision factor — and it cuts both ways. The SFO offers a technology stack built entirely around your family, at a cost (typically $500,000 to $2 million annually all-in for a substantial office) that only makes sense above roughly $500 million in assets. The MFO offers institutional-grade technology at a fraction of the cost, with the tradeoff that you share the platform's roadmap with 39 other families.

For technology vendors, the lesson is simpler: stop selling the same product to both. The SFO buyer wants depth, customization, and white-glove data operations. The MFO buyer wants scalability, billing, compliance, and onboarding speed. They are different customers with different budgets, different decision-makers, and different definitions of success. The vendors that understand this — and build accordingly — are the ones winning.
