Sixty Percent Are Reallocating. Only Thirty-Five Percent Have a Plan for the Office Itself.

UBS’s 2026 survey of 307 family offices found the largest planned reallocation in the report’s history — while succession planning for the office itself lags. Reporting is becoming the infrastructure of transition.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In the spring of 2026, a family council sat down to reshape a portfolio built over forty years. The founder was 71. The portfolio was being reallocated. And the person who knew where every entity’s paperwork lived — all 47 legal entities, trusts, LLCs, partnerships, foundations — was a CFO planning to retire in three years. The family had a plan for the money. It did not yet have a plan for the office that runs the money.

That family is not an outlier. It is the median case in the UBS Global Family Office Report 2026, published May 28, 2026, which surveyed 307 family offices across more than 30 markets with an average net worth of $2.7 billion and average assets under management of $1.3 billion — $627.4 billion of family wealth in total. For the first time, 60% of family offices plan changes to their strategic asset allocation in the next 12 months, the highest level UBS has recorded in more than a decade of running the survey. AI is the leading investment theme, with 65% of offices allocating capital to it, and around half of those investing in data center infrastructure, AI software and platforms, and semiconductors. Alternatives account for 42% of the average portfolio, with private equity at 18% — 8% direct and 10% via funds — while real estate is being trimmed from 11% of portfolios in 2025 to 8% planned for 2026. UBS’s release of the 2026 report carries the survey figures and the resilience theme behind them.

The cohort itself is expanding fast. Deloitte counted 8,030 single family offices worldwide in 2024, up from 6,130 in 2019, and projects 10,720 by 2030. Single family offices manage an estimated $3.1 trillion today, projected to reach $5.4 trillion by 2030, while the total wealth of families operating a family office is expected to grow from $5.5 trillion to $9.5 trillion. The UBS Global Next Generation Report 2026 frames the transition behind those numbers at $83 trillion changing hands over the next two decades. Aleta’s 2026 family office trends review compiles the Deloitte figures and the $83 trillion transfer estimate.

The gap sits between the portfolio and the institution. Fifty-seven percent of family offices now have a wealth succession plan for family members, but only 35% have a succession plan for the family office itself, and just 27% have an organized process to educate and prepare the next generation for future roles. Only 41% report cybersecurity controls. The Bank of America Private Bank 2025 Family Office Study, surveying 335 US decision-makers, found 87% of offices have yet to pass to the next generation, while 59% to 60% expect a leadership transition within ten years. Citi Wealth’s 2026 Global Family Office Report surveyed 351 family offices across 41 countries with an average family net worth of $2.1 billion and found the same pattern from a different sample. Entreprenerd’s analysis of the succession data puts the UBS, Bank of America, and Deloitte figures side by side.

The portfolio is being rebuilt faster than the institution that runs it. That is the story of the family office in 2026.

The honest counterargument

Reallocation intentions are not trades. Surveys overstate action, and the 60% figure measures plans, not flows — a family that tells UBS it plans to change its allocation may spend the year debating the change instead. The UBS sample is UBS clients, not the universe, and the AI allocation at 65% may be thematic herding at the top of a cycle rather than a durable conviction. The succession gap, meanwhile, is chronic rather than new: the share of offices with a plan for the office itself has been roughly stable for years, which suggests the 35% is a structural feature of founder-led institutions, not a crisis arriving in 2026.

Those caveats are real, and they do not change the direction of travel. The offices that are reallocating need consolidated reporting to do it — strategic asset allocation, portfolio risk management, and financial reporting are the most frequently retained internal functions in the UBS survey, precisely because they cannot be outsourced without losing control of the decisions. A reallocation without consolidated reporting is a guess. A succession without a system of record is a handover of spreadsheets.

Why reporting sits at the center

The reporting decision has become a succession decision. The families moving fastest — the 60% reallocating, the 65% allocating to AI, the ones absorbing data center and semiconductor exposures their old systems were never built to track — are the ones whose portfolios have outgrown their tools. The vendors that win are the ones selling continuity: a system of record that survives the founder, the CFO’s retirement, and the next generation’s arrival. The vendors that lose are the ones selling dashboards to a family that needs an institution.

The spreadsheets will persist for years. But their days as the system of record are numbered — not by vendor pitches, but by portfolio complexity, and now by succession timelines as well. The family office that treats reporting as infrastructure will execute the reallocation the survey promises. The one that treats it as software will debate it, one council meeting at a time, while the portfolio moves on without it.
