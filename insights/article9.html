How Long Does It Take to Consolidate Reporting Across a Family’s Accounts?

Vendors quote four weeks. Complex families wait a year. The honest timeline for consolidating family office reporting runs from six weeks for a clean book to eighteen months for a multi-entity estate — and the work that takes the time is not the software.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In September 2026, a principal called her family office with a simple question ahead of a capital call deadline: what is our consolidated net worth, by entity, as of last Friday? The office had signed its reporting-platform contract in March. The answer took six weeks. The capital call did not wait.

The gap between the signature and the first trustworthy report is the least honest number in family office technology. Vendors quote go-live in weeks. Asora says four to six weeks. Masttro says twelve to fourteen. Addepar’s standard onboarding runs sixty to ninety days, with a streamlined route near thirty. All three numbers are true, and none of them describes the same job. The difference is what the family brings to the project: how many accounts, how many entities, how clean the data is, and whether anyone has ever reconciled the whole picture before.

The four stages, and where the time goes

Every consolidation project runs the same four stages, and the vendor playbooks agree on their shape even when they disagree on their length. Discovery and data inventory first: catalog every account, every entity, every field that has to migrate. Then data mapping and migration validation: write the normalization rules for inconsistent formats and prove the history survives the move. Then integrations: connect the live custodian and bank feeds. Then configuration, testing, and go-live: build the entities, permissions, and report templates, reconcile, and cut over. One practitioner playbook — the onboarding guide published by family office platform vendor GCA-FopFo — puts the stages at one to two weeks for discovery, two to four weeks for mapping, one to three weeks for integrations, one to two weeks for configuration, one to two weeks for testing, and one to three days for go-live. Add it up and the project takes six to twelve weeks on paper. The paper is wrong in a specific way: it assumes the inventory exists. Building the inventory is the project.

Stage one: the inventory nobody has

Start with the account census, because no family office knows its own count until it counts. A substantial office holds assets at six to ten custodians and private banks, each reporting in its own format on its own schedule. The bank-account count runs higher than the custodian count: practitioner guides describe offices reconciling 50, 100, or more bank accounts every month, each one a separate statement, a separate login, a separate fee schedule. The extreme case is instructive rather than exceptional. In March 2021, Gabriele Gallotti, founder of the Geneva family office Novum Capital, told Citywire his firm — managing CHF 1.6 billion for 14 families — was dealing with about 220 banking relationships across more than 30 jurisdictions. Deloitte counted 8,030 single family offices worldwide in 2024 and projects 10,720 by 2030. Every one of them adds accounts faster than it closes them, because opening an account takes a signature and closing one takes a committee decision, a tax review, and a conversation with a banker who would rather keep the relationship.

Discovery is where the calendar first slips. The inventory turns up accounts nobody remembered, entities with no clear owner, cost basis that was never recorded, and the same security held under three different identifiers at three custodians. Data mapping is budgeted at two to four weeks on the assumption that the fields to map have been found. Finding them — the field-mapping log, the normalization rules, the decisions about which source wins when two custodians disagree — is the work that consumes the first month, and it is work the vendor cannot do alone. It needs the family’s own staff, and the family’s own staff are the people already producing the quarterly report by hand.

Stage two: the feeds

Each custodian feed is its own negotiation. A direct feed needs a letter of authorization, portal credentials, and a banker’s sign-off, and each of those steps waits on a different person’s calendar. Masttro advertises more than 750 direct data-feed connections. The alternative is aggregation through a third party — Plaid, MX, Finicity, or Quiltt — and the honest evidence on how long that takes comes from a vendor’s own case study. Elk Crest Management, a family office managing investments across Charles Schwab, Fidelity, and other institutions, set out to replace manual quarterly reporting with real-time automation. Integrating a single aggregator directly took its team three months of engineering work, and each additional provider made the codebase harder to maintain. With Quiltt’s unified API, the firm reached a production-ready application with comprehensive coverage in three months — the same three months, but for the whole job instead of one integration. The lesson cuts both ways: direct integrations are slow, and the aggregator shortcut still takes a quarter.

Stage three: the alternatives lag

Then comes the stage no project plan can accelerate, because the family does not control its calendar. A fund does not send a daily holdings feed; it sends a quarterly capital account statement, often as a PDF, with a net asset value already 45 to 90 days stale. K-1s arrive as dense PDFs, and extracting what matters — ordinary income, capital gains, state allocations — is a document-processing problem, not a reporting problem. Canoe Intelligence, the document layer most platforms lean on, retrieves from more than 3,000 general-partner and administrator portals, which is the scale of the problem in one number. The first consolidated report will carry stale NAVs, and the honest platforms disclose the staleness rather than bury it. That is not a failure of the project. It is the nature of private assets.

Stage four: reconciliation and the first report

The last stage is where months become months. The offices that produce a trustworthy consolidated report typically dedicate one to three full-time professionals to data integrity, and the first report is their audit: every break investigated, every position tied to a statement, every entity total proved against its parts. The GCA-FopFo playbook recommends a four-to-eight-week data audit before go-live, and user-acceptance testing of one to two weeks after configuration — and those are the stages most often compressed when the calendar slips, which is exactly backwards, because they are the stages that catch the errors the first report will otherwise publish. Addepar’s independent guides put standard onboarding at sixty to ninety days and six to twelve months for a complex book. This site’s own evaluation put the honest figure higher still: implementations routinely take 12 to 18 months for a complex family structure, not the three to six months vendors project. The first report takes months because it is the first time anyone has proved the numbers tie. Nothing in the project is slower than that proof, and nothing in it matters more.

Why the second report takes days

Here is the part the vendors get right, and the reason the quoted timelines are not lies so much as descriptions of a different moment. Once the mappings exist, the security master exists, and the reconciliation rules exist, the second report is incremental: feeds update daily, breaks arrive as exceptions, and the alternatives roll forward on their own quarterly rhythm. The fixed cost is the first build. The marginal cost is small. A pilot makes the same point in miniature: a staged pilot across a subset of accounts typically runs four to eight weeks, and the full deployment across every account and integration takes several months after it. The families that report the shortest timelines are almost always the ones that ran the pilot first — they paid the discovery cost on ten accounts instead of a hundred, and the full rollout inherited mappings that already worked.

The first report is a construction project. The second report is a habit. The price of the first is measured in months; the price of the second is measured in days.

The honest counterargument

There is a real case for going slow on purpose, and it is stronger than the vendors admit. A wrong consolidated number is worse than a late one: a committee that allocates capital on a net-worth figure with a duplicated entity or a mis-mapped fund has not saved time, it has manufactured confidence. Rushing bakes bad mappings into the security master, and a bad mapping discovered in year two has to be unwound across two years of history. The pilot-first approach — four to eight weeks on a subset of accounts, a data audit before go-live, user-acceptance testing with a signed reconciliation — is not caution for its own sake. It is the cheapest way to buy the first report.

The counter to the counter is equally real, and it is the family’s own responsibility. Offices that arrive with a clean entity chart, signed letters of authorization, and a named owner for every account go live inside the quoted window; offices that discover their data during implementation pay for the discovery in months. The timeline is not something the vendor does to the family. It is something the family’s own record-keeping has already decided, and the implementation merely reveals it. Both statements are true, and they point to the same conclusion: the schedule is set before the contract is signed, in the state of the data the family already holds.

What to demand before signing

Put the timeline in the contract, not in the sales deck. Demand the pilot scope in writing — which accounts, which entities, which reports — and the feed list with named custodians, not a count of connections the vendor supports somewhere. Demand the historical migration depth: how many years of transactions come across, and who validates them. Demand user-acceptance criteria with a signed reconciliation report, not a demonstration. Run the test this site’s Addepar evaluation proposed: hand the vendor three capital account statements, a K-1, and the family’s entity chart, and ask for a consolidated view with look-through and a defensible IRR within a week. And ask for the reference client with the same entity count, not the same assets under management. The first report will still take months. The difference is that the family will know which months, and why, before the clock starts.
