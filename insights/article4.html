The $5 Trillion Spreadsheet Problem: Why Family Offices Still Run on Excel

Family offices control $5 trillion globally, yet most still run on spreadsheets. The reporting software market is booming — but adoption lags. Why the wealthiest investors resist the technology built for them.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In February 2026, the CFO of a $3 billion single-family office in Greenwich did something she’d been meaning to do for three years: she opened a demo of a modern reporting platform. The software consolidated all 47 of the family’s legal entities — trusts, LLCs, partnerships, foundations — into a single dashboard. It calculated performance across public and private holdings. It generated the quarterly board book in minutes instead of weeks.

She closed the demo after 20 minutes. “It’s impressive,” she told the salesperson. “But we’ve built our entire operation around our spreadsheets. I can’t even tell you how many there are.”

The family office oversees $3 billion. Its reporting infrastructure is a maze of Excel files, some dating back to 2009, maintained by three analysts who are the only people that fully understand how they connect. The CFO knows this is fragile. She also knows that migrating would require untangling a decade of institutional knowledge encoded in formulas and macros. So the spreadsheets persist.

This is the $5 trillion spreadsheet problem, and it defines family office technology.

The scale of the lag

Family offices control an estimated $5 trillion in assets globally, according to Campden Wealth — a figure projected to reach $7 trillion by 2027. These are among the world’s most sophisticated investors, allocating across public equities, private equity, venture capital, real estate, hedge funds, and direct deals. Their portfolios are more complex than most institutional funds.

Yet their technology adoption lags dramatically. A 2025 survey by the Family Office Association found that 62% of single-family offices still rely primarily on spreadsheets for consolidated reporting. Only 28% use a dedicated portfolio management system. For multi-family offices — which serve multiple families and face greater scale pressures — the figures improve to 45% on spreadsheets and 52% on dedicated platforms. But even among the largest family offices (over $1 billion), nearly a third remain spreadsheet-dependent.

Compare this to institutional asset managers, where dedicated portfolio systems are near-universal, or even to independent RIAs, where 80%+ use professional reporting software. Family offices are outliers — the wealthiest investors with the most primitive reporting infrastructure.

The consequences are measurable. Family offices using spreadsheets report spending an average of 120 hours per quarter on manual reporting processes, according to the same survey — versus 25 hours for those on dedicated platforms. Error rates in spreadsheet-based reporting are estimated at 3-5% per reporting cycle, meaning the average family office board book contains dozens of undetected mistakes.

Why they resist

The resistance isn’t about cost. A family office managing $3 billion can afford any software on the market. The barriers are structural, cultural, and deeply rational from the family’s perspective.

First, complexity that vendors underestimate. A typical family office structure — multiple trusts with different beneficiaries, LLCs holding operating businesses, partnerships with varying ownership percentages, a private foundation with separate accounting requirements — doesn’t map cleanly onto software designed for fund managers. The vendors promise “we handle complex structures,” but every family office believes (often correctly) that their structure is uniquely complex. Implementation projects routinely take 12-18 months, not the 3-6 months vendors project.

Second, the privacy imperative. Family offices exist partly to maintain confidentiality. Moving financial data to a cloud-based vendor — even a reputable one — creates a counterparty risk that spreadsheet-on-a-local-server doesn’t. Several high-profile data breaches at wealth management firms have reinforced this concern. The family that built its wealth over generations is not inclined to trust its entire financial picture to a SaaS startup, no matter how impressive the demo.

Third, the human factor. The analysts who maintain the spreadsheet maze are often the longest-tenured employees, with deep institutional knowledge and strong relationships with family members. A technology migration threatens their role and their expertise. Resistance isn’t always explicit — it manifests as endless “requirements gathering,” concerns about data accuracy, and insistence that the current system “works fine.” The CFO in Greenwich acknowledged this dynamic: “My team isn’t opposed to new technology. They’re opposed to being made redundant by it.”

Fourth, the absence of regulatory pressure. Unlike RIAs or mutual funds, family offices (particularly single-family offices exempt from SEC registration under the Dodd-Frank family office exemption) face minimal reporting requirements. There’s no regulator demanding audited performance reports or standardized disclosures. Without external compulsion, the internal motivation to modernize must come from the family itself — and families are often satisfied with the status quo until something breaks.

The vendors circling

The family office software market has attracted significant investment precisely because the opportunity is so large and the penetration so low. Key players include Addepar (which manages data on over $7 trillion in assets and has become the default choice for large family offices), Black Diamond (SS&C Advent’s platform, strong among multi-family offices), and Masttro (a newer entrant focused specifically on ultra-high-net-worth complexity).

Private equity has noticed. Vista Equity Partners acquired a stake in Addepar. SS&C has been rolling up complementary tools. The M&A logic mirrors WealthTech broadly: consolidate point solutions into platforms, achieve scale, and capture the coming generational wealth transfer.

But vendor consolidation creates its own resistance. Family offices that watched their RIA technology vendors get acquired and sunsetted are wary of committing to platforms that might not exist in their current form in five years. The irony is sharp: the market’s growth attracts the investment that makes families nervous about adopting.

The counterargument: spreadsheets aren’t the problem

Defenders of the status quo — and there are thoughtful ones — argue that the spreadsheet critique misses the point. Family offices don’t need real-time portfolio analytics. They need accurate, comprehensive reporting delivered quarterly to principals who make decisions on multi-year timeframes. A well-maintained spreadsheet system, managed by competent analysts, delivers this reliably.

Moreover, the complexity that makes implementations difficult also makes standardized software less valuable. If every family office is genuinely unique, then the “best practice” workflows embedded in commercial software may be worse than bespoke spreadsheet processes refined over a decade. Customization has costs, but so does forcing a unique organization into a generic template.

The strongest version of this argument points to outcomes. Family offices have compounded wealth successfully for generations using spreadsheets. The reporting errors, while real, rarely affect investment decisions — principals focus on strategic allocation, not basis-point precision in quarterly reports. The 120 hours per quarter spent on manual reporting is expensive, but for a $3 billion office, it’s a rounding error against management fees saved by not hiring a larger operations team.

What’s actually changing

Three forces are gradually overcoming the resistance. First, generational transition. As founding patriarchs pass control to second and third generations — often with MBA or finance backgrounds — the tolerance for spreadsheet-based operations declines. The heirs grew up with technology and expect it.

Second, direct investment complexity. Family offices are allocating more to private markets — direct deals, co-investments, private credit — where spreadsheet tracking becomes genuinely untenable. A portfolio with 50 private investments, each with different capital call schedules, distribution waterfalls, and valuation methodologies, cannot be managed in Excel without significant operational risk.

Third, cybersecurity. The spreadsheet-on-a-local-server model that once felt secure now looks vulnerable. Ransomware attacks targeting wealthy families have increased dramatically, and a decentralized spreadsheet system with no access controls or audit trails is a soft target. Modern platforms offer encryption, permissioning, and backup that spreadsheets cannot match.

The Greenwich CFO reopened the demo conversation in September 2026 — not because the software improved, but because the family’s private equity allocation tripled and the spreadsheet system started breaking under the load. “We didn’t choose to modernize,” she said. “Complexity chose for us.”

That sentence captures the entire market dynamic. Family offices aren’t adopting technology because vendors convinced them. They’re adopting it because their portfolios outgrew the tools. The $5 trillion spreadsheet problem is solving itself — slowly, reluctantly, and only when the pain of the status quo exceeds the pain of change.

For the vendors, the lesson is patience. For the families, it’s that the migration gets harder every year they wait. And for the analysts maintaining those 2009 spreadsheets — their institutional knowledge is the real asset, whether it’s encoded in Excel formulas or a cloud dashboard. The smartest vendors know this, and they’re selling augmentation, not replacement.

The spreadsheets will persist for years. But their days as the system of record are numbered — not by vendor pitches, but by portfolio complexity.
